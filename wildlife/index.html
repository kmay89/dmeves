How a hand-off would work

  1. Contact first. [Hotline: not set up]. Send a photo and where you are. You get a yes or a referral, and the drop-off address only if it's a yes.
  2. Box it, don't hold it. Cardboard box, towel, air holes, lid on. Dark and quiet. No food or water.
  3. Gloves for anything with teeth. Don't touch raccoons, skunks, foxes, coyotes or bats at all. If one bit or scratched you, wash the wound and call your doctor the same day. Ohio requires animal bites to be reported to the local health department within 24 hours.
  4. Warm it. A bottle of warm tap water in a sock on one side of the box, not touching the animal.
  5. The hand-off takes five minutes. Ohio requires the rehabilitator to record where the animal was found, when it arrived and why it needed care, so you'll be asked those three things. You'd get a case number to follow up on.
Nobody can be charged for bringing in an animal. Rehabilitators in Ohio run on donations; the state charges no fee for the permit and pays nothing for the care.

Records and oversight

Ohio rehabilitation permit holders must keep a record of every animal for two years after its release or final disposition and make it available to the Division of Wildlife, whose officers may inspect the animals and facilities. The Cases tab is that record.

Species & the law

What Ohio and federal law say about each kind of animal a Medina County finder is likely to call about: who may legally care for it, and what the finder may and may not do. Plain-language summary, not legal advice. Citations are on the Sources tab.

The three rules that cover almost everything

Wildlife belongs to the state.ORC 1531.02

No one may take, possess or transport a wild animal in Ohio except as the Revised Code or Division of Wildlife rules allow. There is no general "finder's" exception for keeping an injured or orphaned animal. The lawful move is to get it to a permit holder or to ODNR promptly.

Rehabilitation needs a state permit.OAC 1501:31-25-03 (eff. 3/1/2024) · ORC 1533.08

It is unlawful to rehabilitate any wild animal without a Category I or Category II permit. Category I covers orphans except deer, raccoons, weasels, skunks, mink, badgers, beaver, raptors, canids, bats, mute swans and endangered species. Category II covers all species except deer, coyote, mute swans and endangered species, and ODNR says it also treats rabies-vector species and bobcat as needing the Chief's approval. ODNR requires three years as a Category I holder, or equivalent, before Category II. The permit is free. Records must be kept two years.

Birds are also federal.Migratory Bird Treaty Act · 50 CFR 21.76 · USFWS form 3-200-10b

Nearly every native bird, from robins to hawks to ducks, is federally protected. Rehabilitating one needs a federal migratory bird rehabilitation permit on top of the Ohio permit. Only European starlings, house (English) sparrows and common pigeons are unprotected, and Ohio law says so too (ORC 1533.07).

The rules finders trip over

Nuisance animals have their own rule.OAC 1501:31-15-03

Anyone may live-trap a nuisance wild animal on their own property, but an unlicensed person may hold it no more than 24 hours. A trapped nuisance raccoon, skunk, beaver, coyote, red fox or opossum must be released on site or euthanized; it may not be relocated. Other species must be released outside city or village limits, or on land with the owner's permission. Injured nuisance animals must be euthanized. ODNR's own guidance adds that adult raccoons trapped as nuisances may not be handed to a rehabilitator.

Bites get reported.OAC 3701-3-28

Any bite from a non-human mammal is reported to the local health department within 24 hours. For Medina County that is the Medina County Health Department. A bat in a room where someone was sleeping counts as a possible exposure even with no visible bite: contain the bat, don't release it, and call the health department so it can be tested.

Rabies-vector species are different.ODNR rehabilitator list; ODNR Pub 5475

Ohio treats raccoons, skunks, foxes, coyotes and bats as rabies-vector species. They need Category II plus approval, a rabies-vector workshop, and separate housing. Raccoon-variant rabies is established in northeast Ohio counties east of here, and many rehabilitators in the region decline raccoons for that reason.
